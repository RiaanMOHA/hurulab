Hurulab brand and design strategy

The full plan, from zero to a live public site.

Phase 1

Direction

Decide who we are for and the one lane we hold, before a single design decision is made.

Step 1. Gather everyone's perspective

Lead: brand director. Everyone answers.

Objective. Get every stakeholder answering the same questions in their own words, so the positioning is built on what the team actually believes, not on one person's assumption. This is also the first pass of message mining for the brand voice.

How I do it.

  1. Send the stakeholder prompt to each person and have them answer it end to end, in their own words, and send it back.
  2. Pull their phrasing verbatim into a phrase bank. The exact words a founder uses for good and bad build work are raw material, not paraphrase.
  3. Tag every answer against the draft voice attributes: plain, specific, calm, honest to a fault. Note where the language already sounds like us and where it drifts into hype.
  4. Mark the gaps and contradictions between stakeholders. Those are what the positioning session has to resolve.

Deliverables. One completed answer set per stakeholder, a first trilingual phrase bank, and a one-page summary of where the team agrees and where it splits.

Done when. Every stakeholder has answered in full, and I can point to the exact phrases the brand voice and positioning will be built from.

Step 2. Research the market and competitors

Lead: brand director.

Objective. Prove where every competitor sits and confirm the one open lane, so the positioning is a decision backed by the map, not a hope.

What I decide. Which lane we hold, and the price we can defend on sight.

How I do it.

  1. Hold the four occupied corners and name who owns each: prestige design objects (Block studio, Bito, JL Design), enterprise UX process (AJA, UserXper), engineering scale (Duotify, 5xRuby, the dev houses), and AI-first volume (Tenten, NSS).
  2. State the empty lane plainly: the client uses their own working product before committing, a written governance record, senior design judgment, sold in Chinese to family businesses in succession, at a premium justified by certainty. Confirmed empty in the local research and the international benchmarks.
  3. Set the price anchors buyers carry: templates NT$20k to 80k, half-custom NT$70k to 400k, full-custom NT$300k and up. The NT$900k Jadegia precedent sits above every published anchor, so the demo motion has to justify it in the first minute.
  4. Keep a quarterly watch list: Tenten (may productize a down-market AI offer), NerdTechnic (closest mirror, no design capability, that gap is our opening), FraserTec and the global programmatic wave (closest to the whole model, no design, no proof).

Deliverables. The positioning map, the competitor table, the price ladder, and a single written statement of the empty lane.

Done when. I can name every competitor, place each on the map, and defend the open lane with evidence. The instruction to myself is fixed: do not reposition, sharpen.

Step 3. Positioning and strategy

Lead: brand director.

Objective. Turn the research and everyone's answers into one decision that every later choice serves.

The brand idea. The signed work. An architect stamps drawings. A chef tastes every plate. We sign everything the build produces: one name, personally accountable, with the written record to show it.

The value proposition ladder.

  1. Speed without a team. The best studio in the market books more than a year out. We deliver working software in the time their queue moves one month.
  2. You decide on real software, not pictures of software. Nobody else in this market lets you use your own product before you commit. We checked.
  3. Possible versus impossible. Some of our work would not have been built at all the traditional way.
  4. We show our numbers. Instrumented results, recorded client sentences, and the audit trail, including the times the build was caught guessing and re-run.

The three sharpenings I commit to.

  1. Published results are the second moat. Speed language is already being copied. Nobody publishes case studies with real numbers and recorded client sentences. We publish results and ask the others for theirs.
  2. Take the succession lane by name. Nobody owns 接班. We are the neutral third party through whom a successor modernizes without confronting the founder, and the founder still makes the final call because the live-variants process hands them the choice.
  3. Reframe the teaser against the scam reflex. Never free. Invitation only, built from the prospect's real brand, a numbered handful per quarter. Selectivity turns suspicion into status.

Deliverables. A short written positioning statement, the value proposition ladder, the three sharpenings as committed decisions, and the timeline everyone agrees on.

Done when. One page states who we are for, the open lane, the ladder, and the three sharpenings, and the team has signed it.

Phase 2

The brand and design system

Build the foundation and the four parts every page and every word will obey.

Step 4. What the brand stands for

Lead: brand director.

Objective. Set the foundation every font, color, and line of copy will serve, so the brand pulls one way.

What I decide. The name, the core idea, the personality, the headline, and the promise.

How I do it.

  1. Name. Hurulab, one word, the working mark for now. Never split into two words, never all caps, never all lowercase.
  2. Core idea. The signed work, carried through everything.
  3. Personality and archetype. Test the signed-work idea against the archetypes. Personal accountability, a written record, and craft point to the Creator (mastery through making) or the Ruler (order and standards), with a Sage secondary (truth-telling). Pick one primary, at most one secondary, and use it to settle edge cases, not to overwrite the voice table.
  4. Benchmark. Study the strongest independent creative houses for clarity, for example Sagmeister, and the luxury references that hold a quiet, editorial standard.

Deliverables. A one-page brand platform: name, core idea, personality, archetype line, headline, and promise.

Done when. The platform is short enough to hold in the head, and every following step can be checked against it.

Step 5. Fonts

Lead: design director.

Objective. Three faces, one job each, doing most of the work of looking established without a single decorative move.

The system.

  • Schibsted Grotesk draws display and headlines, weights 600 to 800.
  • Instrument Sans carries body and interface, weights 400 to 600.
  • Sometype Mono marks the record: versions, timestamps, labels, weights 400 to 500.
  • No serif, no italics, no fourth face.

Scale and rules. Display 3rem / 700, h1 2.1rem / 600, h2 1.5rem / 600, h3 1.1rem / 600, body 1rem / 400, small 0.82rem, mono 0.8rem. Tighten tracking as size grows, to -0.04em on display; body tracking zero, leading 1.5. Hierarchy comes from weight and size, never a font swap. No italics and no underline for emphasis.

Deliverables. The type spec and a one-page specimen showing every level in use.

Done when. Anyone can set a heading and a paragraph correctly from the spec without asking me.

Step 6. Color

Lead: design director.

Objective. Warm neutrals and one amber, controlled and confident. Amber appears once per view, on the thing that matters.

The palette.

  • Brand: ink #1a1714, warm sand #e8e2d6, radiant amber #e0742a (one per view).
  • Neutrals: greige 2 #5c554b body text, greige 1 #8f8577 captions and meta, line #c4bbaa strong dividers, hairline #ddd6c8 quiet dividers, sand #efe9dd raised panels, paper #f7f3ea page ground.
  • Semantic, meaning only, never decorative: success #3f7d52, warning #c08a2d, danger #b23b2e, info #3d6b8f.
  • Amber states: hover #c9631f, active #b0551a, focus ring #f4c79e at 2px offset 2px, disabled #cfc7b8.

Rules. Ink on paper clears 13:1, greige 2 on paper 5.5:1. Do not flood a page with amber or use it as a background. No gradients, glows, or decorative shadows.

Deliverables. Color tokens with the state set, plus a contrast audit that names any pair that fails.

Done when. Every text and interface pair passes contrast, and amber appears exactly once in any given view.

Step 7. Logo and page layout

Lead: design director.

Objective. A consistent mark and a layout system that gives every page the same order and makes the brand recognizable on sight.

The mark. The working wordmark is Hurulab set in Schibsted Grotesk 700, tracked tight, black on white. A drawn mark comes later, once the personality is settled, not before. Never split, never all caps, never all lowercase.

The grid and layout.

  • Eight-point grid. Every measure is a multiple of 8px: steps 8, 16, 24, 40, 64, 96, large steps fluid with clamp.
  • Text sits left, ragged right, on a column of 60 to 62 characters, layout max 64rem, never full-bleed text.
  • Left-aligned, asymmetric, editorial. Let space carry the weight. One idea per screen. Real numbers, plainly set.
  • Do not center everything, wrap every block in a card, nest cards, add color or glow, or fill space I can leave empty. Break the grid only to mean something.

The signature. The record line, set in Sometype Mono, marks versions and authorship: Rec 001 / v2.4 / signed RB / 12 days. It is a record, not a stamp.

Deliverables. Logo lockups with clear-space rules, the grid spec, and a set of page templates.

Done when. I can lay out a new page from the templates and it already looks like us.

Step 8. Brand voice

Lead: brand director.

Objective. One voice across three equal languages (zh-TW, English, Japanese), documented so a non-writer can follow it. This has been our recurring weakness: no rules, no banned words, no consistent tone. This step ends that.

How I do it, stage by stage.

  1. Audit and message mining. Sweep every existing piece of our language in all three languages, and harvest the exact words real buyers use. Build the gap summary and the phrase bank (started in step 1).
  2. Set tone coordinates. Place us on the four dimensions: formal versus casual, serious versus funny, respectful versus irreverent, matter-of-fact versus enthusiastic. Likely formal-leaning, serious, respectful, matter-of-fact. Mark the anti-tone on each: respectful but never deferential, serious but never cold.
  3. Message architecture. Run an adjective card sort in all three languages into who we are, who we are not, who we want to be. Force a cut to at most three per cluster, so it is a hierarchy, not a wish list.
  4. Build the attribute table, this but not that:
    • Plain: readability grade 5 to 6. Not dumbed down; no sentence a busy founder must re-read.
    • Specific: one real number per claim. Never "significantly faster," always "nine days faster."
    • Calm: banned words include revolutionary, seamless, leverage, synergy, cutting-edge, world-class. Confidence without volume.
    • Honest to a fault: every case names one thing that went wrong. The audit trail made visible, not self-flagellation.
  5. Multilingual adaptation. Transcreation, not translation, for anything meant to make a buyer feel something; closer translation for specs and disclosures. Validate each language with a native copywriter who understands branding.
  6. Settle the English spelling standard, British or American, and hold it everywhere.

Deliverables. The trilingual voice guide: archetype line, tone map, message architecture, attribute tables with before-and-after examples, banned-word list, readability targets, the transcreation routing table, the phrase bank, and a one-page quick-reference card.

Done when. A stranger can write a proposal opener, a what-went-wrong paragraph, and a handover note in our voice using only the card.

Phase 3

Proof

Turn finished work into published evidence no competitor offers.

Step 9. Case studies

Lead: brand director and design director. This is the proof engine, so it gets the most care.

Objective. Turn finished work into published proof no competitor offers. Every case ends with two numbers and one recorded client sentence, and that fact is itself part of the pitch: we publish results and ask the others for theirs.

The fixed format, in this order.

  1. The numbers. Impact first: time, cost, scope, anything instrumented.
  2. The story. What the client was stuck on, and what we did.
  3. How we worked. Design directed, the build followed, and the discipline that kept it safe: locked source of truth, no invented copy, numbered audits, written handoffs.
  4. The owner's sentence. One recorded client line. Where the client has not yet seen the work, or no verbatim quote exists, say so plainly. Honesty to a fault is the rule; empty applause would break it.

The extraction method for each project. The build extracts the source first: the old work page by page, the interaction guidelines that act as an unbreakable floor, and the reference language of the best in that field. I answer the same fixed question set for every project, covering why it was done, what it proved, and how it proved it, then write each up the same way.

The nine, and their status.

  • Jadegia. 94-year-old jade house, two live brand directions twelve days after kickoff, NT$900k, trilingual, Shopify Plus, 76 color pairs audited. Open item: one owner sentence and one launch number, since the site is not yet live.
  • GKTK. A pitch investors tap through, 17 of 20 steps locked, shown to real investors and money moved. Open item: a verbatim investor quote.
  • Value-add prototype. 28 steps, live map, 3D walkthrough, animated financials, 360 strings in one locked file, all 8 source conflicts decided by a person. Honest claim: possible versus impossible, not cheaper or faster.
  • Moha hub. Scattered decks to a live product hub in three days, five reference sites audited and re-audited. Internal reaction on record, no live-investor use yet.
  • 3D vertical test. A Kumamoto house made walkable on an iPad in four weeks at zero new spend, priced against vendor alternatives the team rejected. Ends on ownership of the skill until a reaction lands.
  • Jadegia Shopify theme. Recoverable from the June meeting notes. Can be drafted in full.
  • Ozu-1. Recoverable from the Kumamoto presentation notes. Can be drafted in full.
  • Kozue. Likely the Moha hub under another name (deploys at kozue-five.vercel.app). Confirm, then merge or split.
  • Haramizu. Not in the notes by that name. Needs the source or the correct name.

The teaser demos, built from case discipline. The invitation-only demos use the same method, built from a prospect's real brand, a numbered handful per quarter. Never free. Every one is recorded so it can become a case.

Deliverables. The case template, the five drafted cases finished, the four open cases extracted and drafted, and the "we show our numbers" line ready to market.

Done when. Every published case follows the format, ends with two numbers and one quote or an honest note of what is missing, and nothing is papered over.

Phase 4

Launch

Hold the whole brand in one place, then go public in the right order.

Step 10. Design guidelines

Lead: design director, with the brand director on voice.

Objective. Hold the whole brand in one place so anyone can use it without guessing.

How I do it. Write down the logo, fonts, colors, layout and grid, and voice, each with examples of right and wrong use, plus the record line convention. Put it all in one place online that anyone can use, for digital work, print, and consulting.

Deliverables. One complete reference, kept current, with a dated revision log and a named owner, mirroring the one-person-accountable rule.

Done when. A non-designer builds a correct page and a correct paragraph from the reference alone.

Step 11. Build and launch the website

Lead: design director, with the web developer.

Objective. Our first public home, launched in the right order: the coming-soon page first, then the full site.

The coming-soon page. A scroll-driven passage: sentences light word by word as you scroll, ending on "Coming soon." and an email capture. Same three faces, black on white, one 2px progress bar. The voice, in order: you built something that took years, it hides behind an ordinary website, you have paid before and got pictures to approve, this time you use the real thing in days, before you commit a cent it makes you money, your team learns to run it, then we go.

Then the full site. Designed to the new brand, built to the prototype-is-the-spec rule, launched with the developer.

Deliverables. The live coming-soon page, then the live full site.

Done when. The first design cycle closes the moment the coming-soon page is live; the full site follows.

Step 12. Set up our public pages

Lead: brand director and design director.

Objective. A real, current footprint in the channels this market checks before it reaches out, closing the gap where we have zero presence today.

How I do it. Stand up a portfolio and the accounts buyers here actually check, keep them current, and use a Golden Pin submission for its built-in business matching, which turns one strong entry into introductions.

Deliverables. A credible set of public pages, kept up to date.

Done when. A buyer or referrer who looks us up finds a real, current presence, not a blank.
