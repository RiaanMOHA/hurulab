\#\# Then, across all of them

Which one draws the progression best, and why. Sapient Codelabs draws the single first-rung-to-build progression best, because the mechanism is unusually tight: a two-week calendar spine (WEEK 1 → WEEK 2 → FINAL SESSION) that terminates in an explicit fork — "Then you decide: with us or without us" — and the sprint's own output \*is\* the next rung's price and plan ("A number for the build, not a range"). It converts a diagnostic into a literal on-ramp, then removes the risk of taking it with "Fully credited if we build together. Yours to keep either way." For a \*multi-rung\* ladder, Sprout Sage draws it best: the overlapping week bands (WEEK 4-6, WEEK 5-10) shingle the phases into one visible timeline instead of six disconnected cards, so the eye reads flow rather than a list.

Most common way of drawing a ladder, and the most unusual. Most common by far: numbered cards (01/02/03) in a vertical stack, each with a duration chip and a one-line promise (ACM, Mindfulware sprints, Sapient's steps, and Sprout Sage all use numbers \+ durations). The most unusual is traztech's "DOOR IT OPENS" device — instead of one spine, every priced entry point carries a footer naming the exact larger engagement it leads into, so the "ladder" is really a fan of many two-step ladders sharing one $0 front door. Xperion's arrow-string title ("Discovery → Definition → Delivery → Optimise") with a signed decision document mandated at each gate is the most unusual \*gating\* treatment.

How the best ones handle the reader who only wants the first step and may never buy the rest. They explicitly bless walking away, which is what makes the first step safe to buy:

\> every artifact (prototype, plan, scope) is yours, usable with any team (Sapient)

\> If you do not, you still keep the deliverable. (traztech)

\> Every sprint ends with a clear decision point — continue, hand off, or walk away. No auto-renewing scope. (Mindfulware)

\> turns an idea into a written brief you could hand to any team (Alderframe, Discovery Sprint)

How they justify charging for a discovery/diagnostic that produces a document, not a product. The consistent move is to reframe the document as the thing that de-risks a much larger spend, and to price it against the cost of getting the build wrong. The strongest single sentence:

\> Hiring a dev team cold is a leap of faith… The sprint replaces that leap with a small, fixed first step: you see exactly how we think, design and plan before committing to a build. (Sapient)

\#\# Then, across all of them

\*\*Which one draws the progression best, and why.\*\* LunoMotion. The mechanism is that it draws the same five steps \*twice at different zoom levels\*: a compact horizontal "engagement arc" with numbered nodes and duration labels gives the whole journey at a glance, then a numbered vertical detail list lets you walk each rung. Every phase repeats an identical scaffold (number → name → duration chip → "what comes out of this phase"), and the recurring promise "Every phase has a clear output and a clear handoff" makes the ladder feel like a chain of locked inputs and outputs rather than a list. Crux Digits is a close second and arguably better for \*pricing\* clarity because it draws the ladder three ways (cards, numbered narrative, side-by-side table) and ties rung 1's price into rung 2 with the credit mechanic.

\*\*Most common way of drawing a ladder, and the most unusual.\*\* Most common: a vertical numbered stack (01, 02, 03…) with a short label, a duration chip, and a deliverable line per step — that's AI-Native, LunoMotion's detail column, Gradion, eflury, Boom. The most unusual is The AI Consultancy: it doesn't "draw" a ladder at all, it presents a flat pricing \*table\* and converts it into a forced sequence with a single sentence ("The Diagnostic Audit is the required first step"). The rule, not the graphic, creates the staircase.

\*\*How the best ones handle the reader who only wants the first step and may never buy the rest.\*\* They make the first rung a self-sufficient purchase and say so:

\> No obligation to proceed. The audit stands on its own as a deliverable. (Gradion)

\> A No-Go Is a Clean Outcome … If automation does not pay off in your case, the report says so — in writing, before you have spent anything on a build. (eflury)

\> The discovery call is where we decide together whether there's a fit. No pitching, no demo… If there's not a fit, we'll say so. (LunoMotion)

\*\*How they justify charging for a discovery/diagnostic that produces a document rather than a product.\*\* The strongest single sentence is Brival's:

Runner-up justifications: eflury frames the document as the thing that prevents wasted spend — "The audit tells you before any build whether your processes and data are ready, and which initiative pays back first." — and Gradion frames the deck as a defensible asset — "A production-grounded business case your board can act on. … Not a range. A number."

\*\*Ownership promises about work made in an early phase, quoted in full, and where they sit.\*\* Several, and this is the dimension your own model leans on hardest:

\> All of it is yours — to use as you see fit, even if we don't move to build together (Boom Consulting — the last bullet of Discovery's "WHAT YOU GET" list, i.e. inside the first phase.)

\> Not access to a platform. Not an ongoing retainer. Yours, permanently. (LunoMotion — but this sits at the \*end\*, in the deliverables of the final phase, describing the built system rather than the discovery artifact.)

Note the pattern: the firms that attach ownership to the \*first paid step\* (Boom, eflury, Crux) put the line inside that step's deliverable list; LunoMotion's strongest ownership language is about the finished build and sits at the bottom of the page.

\*\*What the weak examples get wrong — where a ladder stops feeling like a progression and becomes a price list.\*\* Two failure modes. First, equal-weight rows with no arrow of causation: The AI Consultancy's table lists six packages at visually equal weight, so without the "required first step" sentence it would read as a Chinese menu, not a path. Second, phases that are really one purchase billed in instalments: Weblogic.ie (https://weblogic.ie/growth) presents "Four Phases" (Discovery Brief, Design, Build, Launch) but they're stages of a single €7,995 build with staged invoicing ("Design sign-off triggers the second 30% invoice") — you don't buy Phase 1, decide, then buy Phase 2; you buy the whole thing and it's \*delivered\* in phases. A ladder stops being a progression the moment (a) finishing a rung isn't a real decision point with its own price and its own walk-away, or (b) the steps sit side by side as interchangeable options rather than in a caused order.

\#\# Finally

\*\*What I'd take, and what I'd avoid, for a five-step separately-bought ladder where paid discovery belongs to the client from day one and every price is shown.\*\*

Take, first, LunoMotion's double-zoom drawing: a compact numbered arc of all five steps up top, then a vertical detail block per step using one repeated scaffold (number → name → what you pay → what you get → what you own → what unlocks next). It's the clearest way to show \*five\* rungs without the page feeling like a list. Take, second, Crux Digits' habit of restating the ladder as a side-by-side comparison table after the narrative — a reader deciding between "just discovery" and "the whole thing" wants to scan rows. Take, third, the self-sufficiency language from Gradion/eflury/Boom and put it on \*every\* rung, not just the first, since each of your steps is bought alone: some version of "stands on its own — yours whether or not you continue." Take, fourth, and most important for you: put the ownership promise \*inside the discovery step's deliverable list\*, the way Boom and Crux and eflury do — "everything produced here is yours from day one, usable with us or anyone else" — rather than deferring an ownership statement to the final build the way LunoMotion does. Your differentiator is ownership at the \*start\*, so it must sit at the top of the page, on step 1, not at the bottom on step 5\.

Avoid, first, the instalment-disguised-as-ladder trap (Weblogic): if a price is really "30% now, 30% at sign-off", it's one sale, and readers can smell it. Keep each of your five a genuine, separately-invoiced decision. Avoid, second, hiding the numbers behind "fixed price, set on the call" (LunoMotion, Boom) — you've committed to open pricing, which is a real edge none of the sequence-strongest examples fully use; publish a number or a clear floor on all five. Avoid, third, the flat equal-weight table with no arrow of causation (The AI Consultancy). A table is a fine *supporting* view, but if it's your only view, five separately-bought steps collapse into a menu and the reader loses the sense that finishing one is what earns the next. If you use a table, keep it as the second view, underneath a drawn sequence, and let one column or row carry a visible "you are here → next" cue so causation survives the grid. And avoid burying the walk-away permission in fine print — say plainly on each rung that the client can stop, keep everything, and take it elsewhere, because that candor is what makes a paid first step feel safe rather than like a bait step into a bigger commitment.

**Is there a way of drawing this that none of them tried?**

Yes — and it maps unusually well to your specific model. None of the eight drew the ladder as a **receipt or ownership trail** that visibly accumulates as you climb. Every example draws *time* (durations, weeks, "month 4") or *scope* (bigger deliverables), but none draws *what the client already owns* growing at each rung. Because your whole edge is "everything made in paid discovery belongs to the client from day one," you could draw a vertical staircase where each step, once passed, leaves behind a small "yours now" marker — the roadmap after step 1, the prototype after step 2, the source after step 3 — so that scrolling down, the reader watches their own asset pile grow independently of whether they buy the next rung. The progression's forward motion would come not from "unlocking" the next paid step (the SaaS instinct) but from *accumulating owned artifacts*, which reframes each purchase as buying a thing you keep rather than renting access to a process.

A second untried move: none of them showed the ladder from the **skip point** — i.e. drawing, at each rung, the explicit fork "stop here and here's exactly what you walk away with" versus "continue and here's what's added." Boom gestures at this with its "SCOPE VARIATIONS" (Discovery only / Full build / partnership), but it's a separate section, not drawn into the ladder itself. Building the exit into every rung — a two-branch node at each step — would make the "each step is bought and delivered before the next begins, and you owe nothing further" promise *visible* rather than asserted, which is the one thing the confident, sequence-strong examples (LunoMotion, AI-Native) never quite let you see.

That completes the analysis. To recap what you now have: six full published-price ladders (Crux Digits, AI-Native Agency, The AI Consultancy, Gradion, eflury, LunoMotion) plus two supporting cases (Boom Consulting for progression-and-ownership, Brival for the contrarian "never credited" stance). The clearest per-step *ownership at day one* language came from Boom, eflury, and Crux Digits; the clearest *credit-toward-next* mechanic from Crux Digits and eflury; the best *drawing* of the sequence from LunoMotion; and the sharpest *justification for charging for a document* from Brival.
