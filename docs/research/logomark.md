# What makes a good logomark

Deep research run 17 August 2026 at the owner's instruction, after three rejected proposal
rounds. Five search angles, the top sources fetched and read, and 32 extracted claims put
through adversarial verification: 29 upheld, 3 refuted, and the 3 refutations cover two
claims, listed at the end, because what the checks killed matters as much as what they kept. This is research about
logo design, not about hurulab; the evidence rule bars it from ever being cited as a fact
about the company.

## 1. Three tests, in a fixed order: appropriate, distinctive, simple

Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv's canonical test, stated in *Identify* (2011) and repeated by
Sagi Haviv in interviews: a mark must be appropriate in character to the organization,
distinctive enough to be remembered and described after one or two viewings, and simple in
form. A logo is judged by whether it functions as an identifier, never by taste. Haviv pushes
distinctiveness furthest: a strong mark is "unusual, even awkward", and the core design
problem is holding that against simplicity.

## 2. Silhouette first, color last

Haviv, verbatim: "We design in silhouette, right? We don't even touch colour until we've
found a distinctive own-able silhouette that also feels appropriate." Rand set the same
baseline a generation earlier: a mark must be reproducible in one color and at exceedingly
small sizes. Tom Geismar: simple, concept-driven marks reproduce everywhere; marks that
depend on shading and detail fail. Small size was a deliberate design target for the firm,
not a byproduct: the reduced NBC peacock, and Showtime drawn to scale from one centimetre to
thirty metres.

## 3. Meaning is not designed in; it accrues

Rand: a logo takes its meaning from the quality of the thing it represents, not the other way
around, and literal subject matter barely matters (Mercedes is not automotive, Lacoste's
alligator has nothing to do with clothes). Ivan Chermayeff: a logo must work without
explanation. The Chase octagon (1960) and the National Geographic rectangle carried no
literal meaning and became iconic through decades of consistent use. The working consequence:
meaning belongs in the brief, where it steers appropriateness and specificity; the viewer is
never required to decode it.

## 4. Why minimal geometric marks go generic, and the escape

"Blanding" is a named, documented formula in tech branding: a made-up name, a clean
sans-serif, generous white space, cheerful illustration, often no logomark at all, and
vibrant colors with purple and turquoise the most common. A purple geometric identity
therefore starts inside the category wallpaper. The documented escape is specificity, not
styling: find a genuinely distinctive characteristic of the company, even an unflattering
one, own it, and build the identity around it. Rand's version: simplicity is the outcome of a
sound idea worked hard, and a designer who chases the style without the idea lands on the
generic. One caveat on sourcing: the blanding formula rests mainly on Thierry Brunfaut's
Fast Company piece, with Bloomberg and Base Design echoing rather than independently
establishing it.

## 5. The process the masters actually run

Research before drawing; pencil before computer; the black silhouette refined before any
color; a preliminary trademark search before anything is shown. The designers eliminate the
options themselves, by consensus, before the client sees anything: Rand presented Steve Jobs one mark
for NeXT inside a written case of roughly one hundred pages, including the rejected
precursors and why each failed. Haviv presents finished candidates only, in person, mounted
in realistic applications, which reversed the client's initial rejection of the Armani
Exchange mark. None of them shows a work-in-progress pile, because early designs are too
fragile to survive committee judgment.

## 6. Tests to run before any judging

At 16 and 32 pixels (favicon sizes); in pure monochrome and reversed light-on-dark; the
squint test for a readable core silhouette; and the memory test, showing the mark cold to
someone and asking them to sketch or describe it five minutes later. A mark that relies on
color to separate its elements fails one-color reproduction. The identity also needs a
variant system (icon alone, with wordmark, monochrome), because devices allocate different
spaces.

## What the verification refuted

- "A mark must be simple to last" as a universal law: the quote is real, but detailed marks
  with long lives (Porsche's crest) refute the universal form. Simplicity is the strong
  default, not a law.
- "Appropriate, flexible, original" as the firm's judging criteria: that line is Ivan
  Chermayeff describing memorable identities in a foreword. The firm's canonical test is
  appropriate, distinctive, simple, as in section 1.
