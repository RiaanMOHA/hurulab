# The moat check and the cost reveal

Two ideas for the discovery pages, cut down from a longer brief on 12 August 2026. Both were
built into `concepts/proposal-d-the-moment.html` the same day.

**What this file is.** The reasoning behind the two ideas, kept because it says what would make
them wrong. It is not evidence about hurulab and nothing here goes on a page as fact.

---

## Idea A. The moat check

Ask what a competitor could not copy. A moat is a benefit plus a barrier: something that makes
money, and something stopping a rival taking it away.

**For hurulab's clients**, traditional and often family-owned businesses, the moats that matter
are reputation, switching costs, craft knowledge, cornered local resources, and above all
whether the advantage sits in one person's head or in something transferable. First-generation
advantages, personal networks and hands-on experience, erode across a handover unless they are
made institutional. Converting a person-dependent advantage into a documented one is the work.

**Named non-moats**, to be challenged gently if claimed: being cheapest, a generic location, one
employee who could leave, off-the-shelf technology.

**It must never read as a rejection screen.** A consultancy exists partly to build the moat a
client does not have yet, so screening out those clients rejects the ones it helps most. Every
answer routes to a next step. Three framings, and a reader picking any of the page's four
answers lands in one of them:

- **Strong moat.** You have something real. Make it visible and make sure it survives a handover.
- **Latent moat.** The material is there and customers cannot see it yet. Likely the best-fit
  client.
- **Little material.** Framed gently, never as failure. Offer a small paid engagement to find
  the edge.

**Constraints.** No visible score. No rejection state. Conversational, not a form: heavy scoring
reads as distrust to relationship-oriented owners.

## Idea B. The cost reveal

Let the reader work out the cost of doing it themselves. Discovering a number is more persuasive
than being told it.

**What makes the naive version fail:**

- A generic build-cost calculator already exists in several competing forms.
- The insider term for it is developer language. Traditional business owners do not use it, so a
  hook depending on it attracts developers, who are not the buyers.
- "This costs more, so hire us" reads as a sponsored comparison and triggers distrust, which is
  expensive for a premium brand.

**The pain underneath is real**, which is why the idea survives at all: cost that cannot be
budgeted, debugging that outlasts building, projects that stall before production, and security
failures in generated code holding customer data.

**So it must**: use plain business language, read as an honest estimate the reader keeps
whatever they decide, lead with judgment and risk rather than arithmetic, and anchor doing it
once against the real cost of doing it twice.

**Constraints.** Never one confident number, always a visible range. The do-it-yourself path is
treated with respect, including saying a simple idea may not need hurulab. Risk is drawn in the
warning color, never the danger color: the tone is calm, not alarmist.

---

## How they connect

The moat check runs first and establishes what the reader has. The cost reveal then references
what they said, by name, rather than making a generic transition. One close, not two.

**The close hands them something they keep**, a summary carrying their own answer back, with the
next step under it rather than as the point of the screen. This is the part that decides whether
the whole flow reads as useful or as a lead-capture funnel, and it was missed in the first build
because it sat buried mid-sentence here.
